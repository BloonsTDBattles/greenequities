Climate Risk in Investment Portfolios

By Daniel Denisovich • December 10, 2025 • 10 min read
← Back to Articles

Climate change is no longer a distant threat—it's a present reality reshaping global economies and financial markets. For investors, understanding and managing climate risk has become essential to protecting and growing wealth over the long term. This article explores how climate change impacts investment portfolios and strategies to build climate-resilient investments.

Understanding Climate Risk

Climate risk refers to the potential financial impacts of climate change on investments. These risks fall into two main categories: physical risks and transition risks.

Physical Risks

Physical risks arise from the direct impacts of climate change, including:

  • Acute risks: Extreme weather events like hurricanes, floods, wildfires, and droughts that can damage property, disrupt operations, and destroy assets
  • Chronic risks: Longer-term shifts in climate patterns, such as rising sea levels, changing precipitation patterns, and temperature increases that affect business operations and asset values

These physical impacts can severely affect companies' operations, supply chains, and profitability. Coastal real estate faces flooding risks, agricultural businesses struggle with changing growing conditions, and insurance companies face mounting claims from climate-related disasters.

Transition Risks

Transition risks emerge from society's shift toward a low-carbon economy:

  • Policy and regulatory changes: Carbon taxes, emissions trading systems, and stricter environmental regulations
  • Technological disruption: Advances in renewable energy, electric vehicles, and energy storage displacing fossil fuel-dependent industries
  • Market shifts: Changing consumer preferences and investor sentiment favoring sustainable products and companies
  • Reputational impacts: Brand damage for companies perceived as climate laggards

How Climate Risk Affects Different Asset Classes

Equities

Stock prices can be significantly impacted by climate risk. Companies in carbon-intensive industries like fossil fuels, heavy manufacturing, and traditional utilities face both physical and transition risks. Meanwhile, companies positioned to benefit from the green transition—renewable energy, clean technology, sustainable agriculture—may see enhanced growth opportunities.

Fixed Income

Climate change affects bond markets through credit risk. Municipalities vulnerable to sea-level rise or extreme weather may face downgrades. Corporate bonds from carbon-intensive industries could see higher default risk as the economy transitions. Conversely, green bonds financing climate solutions have grown into a major asset class.

Real Estate

Property investments face direct physical risks from flooding, wildfires, and extreme weather. Buildings with poor energy efficiency may lose value as regulations tighten and operating costs rise. Location matters enormously—coastal properties and those in wildfire zones face particular challenges.

Commodities

Climate change creates both winners and losers in commodity markets. Fossil fuel demand faces long-term structural decline, while metals needed for renewable energy infrastructure and batteries may see sustained demand growth.

Measuring Climate Risk in Your Portfolio

Several tools and metrics can help assess your portfolio's climate exposure:

Carbon Footprint: Measures the total greenhouse gas emissions associated with your investments, typically expressed in tons of CO2 equivalent per million dollars invested.

Fossil Fuel Exposure: Percentage of your portfolio invested in companies deriving revenue from fossil fuel extraction, processing, or distribution.

Climate Value-at-Risk (Climate VaR): Estimates potential losses under different climate scenarios, accounting for both physical and transition risks.

Temperature Alignment: Assesses whether your portfolio's emissions trajectory aligns with global temperature targets like the Paris Agreement's 1.5°C or 2°C goals.

Strategies for Building Climate-Resilient Portfolios

1. Diversification with Climate Awareness

Traditional diversification remains important, but consider climate risk across your holdings. Don't concentrate investments in climate-vulnerable sectors or geographies. Balance exposure across industries positioned differently for the climate transition.

2. Positive Screening for Climate Leaders

Actively seek companies leading their industries in climate performance. Look for strong carbon reduction targets, renewable energy adoption, and climate risk disclosure. These leaders often demonstrate superior management quality and forward-thinking strategy.

3. Thematic Climate Investing

Consider allocating to themes positioned to benefit from climate action:

  • Renewable energy generation and storage
  • Electric vehicles and charging infrastructure
  • Energy efficiency technologies
  • Sustainable agriculture and alternative proteins
  • Water management and conservation
  • Circular economy and recycling

4. Engagement and Stewardship

As a shareholder, you have the power to influence corporate behavior. Support shareholder resolutions calling for better climate disclosure and ambitious emissions reduction targets. Many asset managers now offer engagement strategies focused on pushing companies toward better climate practices.

5. Stress Testing

Regularly assess how your portfolio would perform under various climate scenarios. What happens if carbon prices rise significantly? How would extreme weather events affect your holdings? This forward-looking analysis helps identify vulnerabilities before they materialize.

The Opportunity Side of Climate Risk

While climate change poses significant risks, it also creates substantial investment opportunities. The global transition to a low-carbon economy will require massive investment—an estimated $100+ trillion over the coming decades. This transformation will create winners in numerous sectors.

Early investors in climate solutions have already seen strong returns. The clean energy sector has consistently outperformed fossil fuels in recent years. As the transition accelerates, companies providing solutions will likely continue benefiting from supportive policies, falling costs, and growing demand.

Don't Wait to Act

The worst mistake investors can make regarding climate risk is assuming it's too far in the future to matter. Climate impacts are accelerating, and markets are increasingly pricing in these risks. Portfolio repositioning takes time, and those who wait may find themselves locked into declining assets or buying into climate solutions at higher valuations.

Start by assessing your current climate exposure, set goals for reducing climate risk while capturing climate opportunities, and develop a plan to gradually shift your portfolio. Even small steps today can significantly improve your long-term investment outcomes.

Conclusion

Climate risk is financial risk. The evidence is overwhelming that climate change will reshape the global economy, creating winners and losers across all asset classes. Investors who understand these dynamics and position their portfolios accordingly will be better prepared for the challenges ahead while capturing the opportunities of the clean energy transition.

Building a climate-resilient portfolio isn't about sacrificing returns—it's about recognizing where the world is headed and investing accordingly. The transition to a sustainable economy is the defining economic shift of our era. Make sure your portfolio is positioned on the right side of history.
